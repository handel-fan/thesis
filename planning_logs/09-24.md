Given the thing that keeps happening with your professor, I would **stop optimizing for “cool compiler feature” and optimize for “there is an identifiable new algorithm in the middle of the thesis.”**

After checking the literature, three of the ideas stand out much more than the rest.

### 1. Backward decomposition of a high-level error contract into requirements on a lowering

This is the one I would investigate hardest.

The statement would be something like:

> **We introduce a method for deriving sufficient numerical constraints on the operations introduced by a compiler lowering from an error requirement on the original high-level operation.**

Concrete problem:

```text
High level:

    C = matmul(A, B)

Requirement:

    error(C) <= ε
```

Then one possible lowering produces:

```text
loads
multiplications
reductions
casts
vector operations
```

You want an algorithm that takes:

$$
E_{\text{matmul}}\leq\epsilon
$$

and derives something meaningful about the lower-level computation:

$$
C_1(E_{\mathrm{mult}},E_{\mathrm{reduce}},E_{\mathrm{cast}},\ldots)
$$

such that satisfying those conditions guarantees the original contract.

That is **not**:

> Add `error_bound = 1e-4` to an MLIR op.

It is:

> Given a semantic relation between two representations, derive lower-level obligations sufficient to establish a high-level numerical property.

That survives deleting MLIR.

You could write the research problem without mentioning MLIR at all:

> Given a semantics-preserving decomposition \(H\rightarrow L\), derive numerical-error obligations over \(L\) sufficient to establish an error contract over \(H\).

Then MLIR becomes where you test it.

And MLIR gives you an unusually natural experimental setting because progressive lowering really does destroy/change structure. The MLIR Linalg rationale explicitly warns that lowering loses high-level information and motivates preserving structure across transformations. ([MLIR][1])

#### Why I like this given your professor problem

If he says:

> “That's just implementing error analysis in MLIR.”

you have an immediate answer:

> “No. Existing error analysis tells me the error of a given numerical program. My question is how to **derive obligations on a different representation produced by lowering** from a contract stated over the original representation.”

That's a much cleaner distinction.

There is prior work you would absolutely need to distinguish from. PRECiSA already constructs error expressions compositionally through program structure, so merely composing errors is not new. ([Springer Nature Link][2])

And a 2025 MLIR project already introduced separate real-arithmetic and fixed-point dialects and an end-to-end numerical lowering flow. So **“numerical semantics across MLIR levels” alone is already too weak.** ([DOI][3])

Your contribution has to be the **backward derivation/decomposition procedure**.

---

### 2. Cross-level optimization: make high-level and low-level numerical choices jointly instead of independently

This one got **more interesting** after looking at prior work.

Statement:

> **We introduce an optimization method that jointly selects numerical transformations at multiple compiler abstraction levels under a single end-to-end error constraint.**

Imagine:

```text
HIGH LEVEL
matmul
 ├─ algorithm A
 └─ algorithm B

        ↓

MID LEVEL
reduction
 ├─ sequential
 ├─ tree
 └─ compensated

        ↓

LOW LEVEL
arithmetic
 ├─ FP16
 ├─ BF16
 └─ FP32
```

The normal compiler might make these decisions separately.

But suppose:

```text
algorithm A + tree reduction + FP16
```

is bad, while

```text
algorithm A + compensated reduction + FP16
```

is fine.

And perhaps:

```text
algorithm B + ordinary reduction + FP32
```

is slower.

Then you have a coupled search problem:

$$
\min \operatorname{cost}(L_1,L_2,\ldots,L_n)
$$

subject to

$$
E(L_1,L_2,\ldots,L_n)\leq\epsilon.
$$

The research contribution would be the algorithm for navigating that **hierarchical lowering space**.

This is attractive because there is evidence that the phase interaction itself is a real problem.

Anton already combines rewriting and mixed-precision selection under an error constraint—but notably, its authors say jointly optimizing the two spaces would require an infeasible exhaustive search, so they deliberately separate rewriting from precision tuning. ([arXiv][4])

The Herbie researchers independently describe essentially a **phase-ordering problem** between numerical optimization and subsequent compilation: optimizing a numerical expression without knowing the eventual implementation platform can lead to the wrong choices. ([UW PLSE][5])

That gives you a possible opening:

> Existing numerical optimizers generally optimize one representation or separate interacting optimization stages. MLIR exposes several explicit abstraction levels. Can we make coupled decisions across two of those levels without exhaustively enumerating every lowering combination?

That sounds considerably more like a thesis than:

> “I implemented mixed precision in MLIR.”

#### Scope it aggressively

Not:

```text
Tensor → Linalg → SCF → Vector → LLVM → GPU
```

all at once.

Something like:

```text
Linalg-level algorithm/decomposition choice
                 +
Vector/Arith precision/reduction choice
```

for 2–3 operations.

Then your thesis is the **search/optimization method**, and the implementation demonstrates it.

This is probably the strongest competitor to #1.

---

### 3. Numerical provenance with targeted repair

This one is weirder, but precisely because it is weirder, it may escape the giant pile of existing mixed-precision work.

Statement:

> **We introduce a method for attributing portions of an end-to-end numerical error bound to specific compiler transformations and use those attributions to identify the minimum-cost set of transformations that must be revised when an error contract is violated.**

Suppose:

```text
original

 ↓ FP16 conversion
 ↓ reassociation
 ↓ vector reduction
 ↓ approximate exp

final error bound = 1.7e-3
required          = 1.0e-3
```

A naive compiler response might be:

```text
Oops.

Use FP32 everywhere.
```

Your method might determine:

```text
FP16 conversion       contribution / amplification: ...
reassociation         ...
vector reduction      ...
approximate exp       ...

Changing only reduction strategy gets us under 1e-3.
```

So the problem becomes:

$$
\text{Which transformation decisions are responsible for violating the contract?}
$$

and then:

$$
\text{What is the cheapest repair?}
$$

That is a recognizably different algorithmic problem.

I found lots of work on **computing numerical error**, but my searches did not turn up an obvious established compiler framework whose central abstraction is transformation-level numerical provenance followed by minimum-cost repair. That is **not proof of novelty**, but it makes this one worth a serious literature search.

The danger is that:

> “track which pass introduced error”

could instantly become a product.

You need an actual attribution/repair algorithm:

```text
Given:
    transformation dependency graph
    composed error model
    violated bound
    costs of alternatives

Compute:
    minimum-cost set of decisions to revise
```

Now there is something to publish.

---

## A fourth one I would keep around, but below those three: numerically aware compiler phase ordering

This is related to #2 but has a narrower research question:

> **We introduce a method for ordering numerical compiler transformations based on how earlier transformations alter the legality or profitability of later transformations under an error constraint.**

Example:

```text
precision lowering
reassociation
fusion
vectorization
```

Perhaps:

```text
reassociate → FP16
```

satisfies the constraint,

while:

```text
FP16 → reassociate
```

doesn't.

Now you aren't merely selecting transformations. You're solving:

> **In what order should accuracy-affecting transformations occur?**

Herbie's recent architecture discussion explicitly identifies numerical compilation as having a phase-ordering problem, which is encouraging evidence that this is a real problem rather than something we're inventing for thesis flavor. ([UW PLSE][5])

But you would need to narrow it dramatically, because arbitrary compiler phase ordering is already a huge research field.

---

# The ones I would now demote hard

**Sensitivity-guided precision selection:** too crowded. Fine-grained sensitivity and mixed-precision analyses have existed for years, and modern quantization systems also explicitly score operation/layer sensitivity. ([Sage Journals][6])

**Automatic FP16/FP32/FP64 assignment:** definitely crowded. Anton, FPTuner, Precimonious and newer work already occupy this territory. Anton even guarantees a user-specified error bound. ([arXiv][4])

**Joint rewriting + precision:** also already exists. That's almost literally Anton's headline contribution. ([arXiv][4])

**Compositional error summaries:** dangerous as a novelty claim. PRECiSA explicitly describes its analysis as compositional and builds symbolic error expressions compositionally. ([Springer Nature Link][2])

**Automatic input precondition inference:** surprisingly, that one is already directly taken. A 2022 paper presents techniques for automatically synthesizing floating-point input preconditions that guarantee a requested error bound. ([Springer Nature Link][7])

**Error-aware reduction-tree selection:** real and interesting, but the numerical-analysis literature is active enough that I wouldn't choose it casually. There are established error bounds for computational trees and mixed-precision summation, and a July **2026** paper specifically derives error models and characterizes optimal reduction-tree topologies and schedules. ([arXiv][8])

**Generic rewrite/approximation selection inside a production compiler:** also increasingly crowded. Poseidon, published at CGO 2026, instruments programs, generates candidate numerical rewrites, evaluates them, and optimizes performance/accuracy tradeoffs inside a production compiler. ([CGO 2026][9])

**Proof-producing floating-point optimization:** intellectually strong but not empty territory and potentially enormous for a master's thesis. RealCake already integrates proof-producing accuracy analysis into verified floating-point compilation. ([DROPS][10])

---

So given **your exact recurring frustration**, my ordering right now is:

**A. Backward contract decomposition across a lowering**
Most cleanly answers *“what method did you invent?”*

**B. Cross-level co-optimization under one numerical constraint**
Possibly the richest actual compiler thesis, with a clear search/optimization algorithm.

**C. Numerical provenance + minimum-cost repair**
Highest novelty upside from what I've found so far, but also the least established and therefore the riskiest.

**D. Numerical phase ordering**
Potentially excellent if we find a very specific two- or three-pass interaction nobody has solved.

And I would **not** take “numerical-error-aware lowering in MLIR” back to your professor yet. That's still too product-shaped.

I would take something shaped like:

> **We introduce a backward analysis that derives numerical obligations on alternative low-level implementations from an error contract stated on a high-level tensor operation.**

or

> **We introduce a cross-level optimization algorithm that jointly selects high- and low-level lowering decisions under a global numerical-error constraint, avoiding the loss from optimizing each compiler level independently.**

Those are finally statements where the obvious next question is **“how does your algorithm work?”**, rather than **“so you added this feature to MLIR?”**.
